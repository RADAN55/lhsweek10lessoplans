3. Read — “Tamales: A Meal Wrapped in History”

As you read: find each Word Bank word. Note one fact that surprises you. Mark anything you do not understand.

1. A gift you can eat

Imagine a food that is more than 5,000 years old, that soldiers once carried into battle, and that families still make together every December. That food is the tamal (plural: tamales). A tamal is made from masa, a soft dough of ground corn. The masa is spread on a dried corn husk or a banana leaf, filled with meat, cheese, chiles, beans, or sweet fruit, then folded and steamed. When it is finished, you unwrap it like a small present. The word comes from tamalli in Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs, and it simply means “wrapped.”

2. The science inside the masa

Tamales begin with an ancient discovery. Thousands of years ago, people in Mesoamerica learned to soak dried corn in water mixed with ash or lime (a mineral, not the fruit). This process, called nixtamalization, softens the corn, gives it a special flavor, and releases a vitamin called niacin. Without this step, people who ate mostly corn became sick. With it, corn could feed entire civilizations. Every tamal you eat today still depends on this invention.

3. A food for travelers

Long before cars or refrigerators, tamales were the perfect “to-go” meal. Aztec and Maya warriors, hunters, and traders carried them because they were easy to pack and stayed fresh for days. A Spanish priest named Bernardino de Sahagún, who wrote about Aztec life in the 1500s, described markets selling dozens of kinds: tamales with turkey, with frogs, with honey, and even with flowers. Some historians call the tamal one of the world’s first portable foods, thousands of years before the sandwich.

4. One food, many countries

Tamales are not only Mexican. In Guatemala, tamales colorados are wrapped in banana leaves with a red tomato sauce. In El Salvador and Honduras, families make tamales de elote, sweet tamales from fresh corn. In Colombia and Venezuela, a similar dish called the hallaca is stuffed with raisins, olives, and egg. In Peru, tamales verdes are green from cilantro. In the Philippines, which was once connected to Mexico by Spanish trade ships, there is even a tamal made with rice instead of corn.

5. Hot tamales in Mississippi

Tamales also have a home right here. In the early 1900s, Mexican workers came to the Mississippi Delta to pick cotton. They shared their food with African American workers in the fields, and a new tradition was born: the Delta hot tamale. It is smaller, spicier, and simmered in a red broth instead of steamed. Blues musicians sang about them, and today towns like Greenville hold a hot tamale festival every October. A tamal in Mississippi tells a story about two cultures meeting.

6. The tamalada

Making tamales takes many hands, so families often hold a tamalada, a tamal-making party. Grandmothers mix the masa, children spread it on husks, uncles argue about the filling, and everyone tells stories. By the end, there may be 200 tamales ready for Christmas, New Year’s, or the Day of the Dead. The work is the celebration.

7. Why tamales still matter

A tamal carries more than food inside. It carries language, science, memory, and the hands of the people who made it. When families move to a new country, making tamales is one way to keep a piece of home. As one Mexican saying goes, “Al que nace para tamal, del cielo le caen las hojas” — “For the one born to be a tamal, the husks fall from the sky.” In other words, some things are simply meant to be.

Read along after you listen

A quinceañera celebrates a girl’s fifteenth birthday and her step from childhood into young adulthood. It began in Mexico, and today families celebrate it in Central America, the Caribbean, and the United States. The day often begins with a ceremony at church, where the family gives thanks and the godparents offer a blessing. Then comes the party. The quinceañera enters with her court of honor — friends who dance with her. She dances a waltz with her father, uncle, or another important adult. After that, a parent changes her flat shoes for high heels. The shoes symbolize that she is no longer a little girl. Some families also give her a “last doll,” the última muñeca, as a goodbye to childhood. Finally, someone gives a toast, the family cuts the cake, and everyone dances late into the night. Not every family has a quinceañera; some choose a trip, a small dinner, or nothing at all. But for many, it is the biggest party of a lifetime.

4. Genre map — Read and trace the journey

Mariachi — Mexico (Jalisco)

Mariachi began in the state of Jalisco in western Mexico in the 1800s. A mariachi group plays violins, trumpets, a large bass guitar called the guitarrón, and a small guitar called the vihuela. Musicians wear the charro suit with silver buttons. Mariachi is the music of weddings, birthdays, and serenades. Mexican migrants carried it to Texas, California, and later to Mississippi and every state where families gather.

Cumbia — Colombia

Cumbia was born on the Caribbean coast of Colombia, where African, Indigenous, and Spanish people lived together. Its steady rhythm came from African drums, its flute from Indigenous music, and its lyrics from Spanish. In the 1900s cumbia migrated to Mexico, where the accordion became its voice. Today Mexican and Texan cumbia is some of the most popular dance music in the Americas.

Salsa — Cuba, Puerto Rico, New York

Salsa mixes Cuban son and Puerto Rican rhythms with jazz. It grew up in New York City in the 1960s and 70s, when Cuban and Puerto Rican musicians played together in the same neighborhoods. Salsa uses trumpets, trombones, piano, and the clave, two wooden sticks that keep the rhythm. Salsa is fast, bright, and made for dancing with a partner.

Bachata — Dominican Republic

Bachata started in the countryside of the Dominican Republic as guitar music about love and heartbreak. For many years rich people looked down on it. In the 1990s, Dominican musicians in New York made it modern with electric guitar, and today bachata is heard from Santo Domingo to Laurel. It is slower and more romantic than salsa.

3. Read — “The Ofrenda: A Table Full of Memory”

As you read: find each object and what it means.

Every year on November 1 and 2, families in Mexico and across Latin America celebrate Día de los Muertos, the Day of the Dead. It is not Halloween, and it is not scary. It is a time to remember and honor the people who have died — grandparents, parents, friends, even pets. Families believe that on these two nights, the spirits of their loved ones come home for a visit. To welcome them, families build an ofrenda, a table of offerings, in the house or at the cemetery.

Each object on the ofrenda has a job. Bright orange marigolds, called cempasúchil, are placed in paths and arches because their color and strong smell guide the spirits home. Sugar skulls, calaveras de azúcar, are decorated with smiles and the names of the dead; they remind everyone that death is part of life and can even be sweet. Colorful cut paper, papel picado, hangs above the table; the holes let the wind, and the spirits, pass through. Pan de muerto, a round sweet bread with bone shapes on top, feeds the visitors, along with the person’s favorite meal, drink, or candy. Candles light the way. Salt keeps the spirits pure. And in the center sit the photos of the people being honored, so the family can see their faces and tell their stories.

The holiday is more than 3,000 years old. The Aztecs and other peoples of Mexico kept skulls and honored their ancestors long before the Spanish arrived. After the Spanish came, the Indigenous holiday mixed with the Catholic days of All Saints and All Souls, which fall on the same dates. Today Día de los Muertos is celebrated with parades in Mexico City, with family picnics in cemeteries, and with school ofrendas in the United States. Families laugh, eat, sing, and cry a little. They celebrate because, as long as someone remembers you, you are never really gone.
